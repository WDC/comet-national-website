You have a shipment that doesn't fill a trailer. The obvious call is to pay for the space you use and split the rest with somebody else's freight. Most of the time that's right. But not always. There are loads where booking a whole truck, even one you can't fill, comes in cheaper than the partial van you thought was the smart move. Knowing which is which saves you real money.

## How partial van pricing actually works

A partial van shares trailer space with other freight. You pay for the room your pallets take up, roughly the linear feet and the weight, and the carrier fills the rest of the deck with someone else's load. When your shipment is small, dense, and easy to handle, this is almost always the better deal. You're not paying for air.

The math changes when your freight is bulky, long, or heavy for its size. Partial pricing is built around how much space and weight you consume. Push either one and the price climbs fast. A load that eats up most of the trailer's length or most of its legal weight isn't really a partial anymore, even if you only have a handful of pallets.

## The tipping point most shippers miss

Here's the position we'll take: the moment your partial shipment starts consuming more than about half the trailer, you owe it to yourself to price a full truckload beside it. Not because partial is bad, but because the discount for sharing space shrinks as your share grows.

Watch for these signs that a whole trailer might win:

- **Your freight is long or takes up a lot of floor.** Once you're using most of the deck's length, a carrier can't sell the leftover space to anyone. You end up paying for it anyway, just with a partial label on it.
- **You're heavy for your footprint.** If your weight caps out the trailer before your pallets fill it, the carrier can't add more freight on top. That's a truck you've effectively bought.
- **Your freight is fragile, high-value, or awkward to stack.** Shared loads mean handling at cross-docks and other freight riding alongside yours. A dedicated trailer skips the touches and the stacking risk.
- **Your timing is tight.** Partial moves ride a schedule built around consolidation. A full truckload goes when you say go.

## Why fewer touches can beat a lower line-item

The cheapest quote isn't the cheapest shipment. A partial van looks better on the rate sheet, then costs you when the freight gets handled three times, a pallet gets crushed, or the consolidation schedule adds days you didn't plan for. A whole trailer that loads at your dock and delivers direct has none of that. When your freight is delicate or your delivery date is firm, the door-to-door move can be the lower total cost even at a higher headline rate.

This is exactly the kind of question worth asking before you book, not after the bill shows up. The difference between a partial and a full truckload is sometimes smaller than shippers assume, and the full trailer buys you control.

## When partial is still the right answer

We're not telling you to buy a whole truck every time. If your freight is compact, stacks well, and your timeline has some give, a partial dry van is usually the smarter spend. You pay for your space and skip the cost of the empty trailer. The point is to run both numbers instead of defaulting to one.

And if your freight belongs on an open deck instead of inside a van, that's a different conversation. Long, tall, or odd-shaped loads that need strapping or tarping don't fit the van math at all.
