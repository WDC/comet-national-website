You call for a flatbed rate on a load. Your buddy at the plant down the road ships something that looks about the same and pays a lot less. Now you're staring at two quotes wondering who got played.

Usually nobody did. Open-deck pricing isn't a flat sheet. It reflects what the load actually demands from the truck, the driver, and the route. Two loads that look similar on paper can carry very different costs once you account for how they really move. Here's what goes into the number.

## The freight itself

Start with weight and dimensions, because they set the floor.

- **Weight.** A load riding near legal gross weight limits the driver's options and can push you toward specialized equipment. A light load on the same trailer prices differently.
- **Length and width.** Once you're past standard trailer dimensions, you're into oversized territory. That changes everything downstream: permits, routing, and sometimes escorts.
- **Height.** Tall freight affects tarping, bridge and overpass clearances, and legal height limits. A load that clears easily costs less to move than one that has to be routed around low structures.
- **Shape.** A neat, stackable, strap-friendly load is cheaper to secure than something awkward that eats deck space and takes an hour to chain down.

Two loads at the same weight can price far apart if one is a tidy machine base and the other is a sprawling, off-center piece that needs blocking and extra securement.

## Securement, tarping, and handling

Open-deck freight has to be strapped, chained, or tarped so it survives the trip and passes a roadside inspection. That labor is real, and it's part of the quote.

Tarping alone can move a rate. Pulling tarps over a tall or irregular load is heavy, time-consuming work, and some freight has to stay covered against weather the whole way. A load that needs no tarp is simpler and cheaper than one that does. Securement follows the same logic: more tie-down points, more dunnage, and more time on the deck all show up in the price.

## Permits, routing, and escorts

The moment a load goes oversized or overweight, you're dealing with state permits, and each state has its own rules and fees. Routing gets more complicated too. The truck may have to avoid certain roads, bridges, or times of day. Larger dimensions can require pilot cars or escorts.

This is where two "flatbed loads" separate hard. A legal-dimension load rides straight through. An oversized load crossing several states can carry permit costs, a restricted route that adds miles, and travel-time limits that stretch the schedule. Same trailer type, very different job.

## Lane, distance, and market

Where the load starts and ends matters as much as what it is.

- **Distance** is obvious, but the type of miles counts too. A deadhead to reach the pickup, or a lane that leaves the driver stuck in a market with no freight back, gets priced in.
- **Balance of the lane.** Some lanes have trucks fighting for loads. Others have loads fighting for trucks. Open-deck capacity is tighter than dry van in a lot of places, which is exactly why odd freight gets bounced or surcharged elsewhere.
- **Timing.** A tight pickup or delivery window, or a season when deck capacity is short, tightens the number.

## Accessorials and the job around the load

The move includes more than the drive. Does the driver need to wait at a slow dock? Is the pickup a tight residential or construction site? Is there a load and unload requirement, or special equipment on site? These details are why an honest quote asks a lot of questions up front. The goal is a number that holds, not a low teaser that grows once the truck shows up.

Give us the real picture and you'll get a real price:
