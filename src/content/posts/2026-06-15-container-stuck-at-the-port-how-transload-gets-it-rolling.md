Your container cleared customs, but it's still sitting. The steamship line wants it back, the demurrage clock is running, and the drayage window you thought you had just got pushed. Meanwhile the freight inside it has a delivery date someone is holding you to. This is one of the most expensive kinds of stuck: everything is technically fine, and nothing is moving.

The fix usually isn't another phone call to the port. It's a transload.

## What transload actually does

Transloading means taking freight out of one mode and putting it into another. In this case, that's pulling your goods out of an ocean container and reloading them onto over-the-road trucks. The container goes back to the port empty, your demurrage and per diem stop piling up, and your product keeps moving toward where it actually needs to go.

That sounds simple, and the concept is. The value is in doing it fast and doing it clean, close to the port, before the charges eat your margin.

Comet runs an Atlanta-based transload facility built for exactly this. A container comes in, we break it down, and we load it out on the right equipment for where it's headed. If the freight belongs on a flatbed because it's long or heavy, it goes on a flatbed. If it's palletized dry goods bound for three different states, we load it accordingly. You're not locked into whatever the container dictated.

## Why the container itself is the bottleneck

An ocean container is great for crossing water and terrible for the last leg of a domestic move. A few reasons it holds you up:

- **The clock.** Demurrage runs while the box sits at the terminal. Per diem runs once you pull it. Both are charges nobody approved, and they grow every day the container stays in play.
- **The equipment.** A 40-foot ocean container isn't the same as a 53-foot dry van. You lose usable space, and drayage rates and chassis fees stack on top of your line-haul.
- **The routing.** One container often holds freight going to more than one destination. Trying to deliver straight from the box means dragging everything to every stop.

Transload breaks all three of those. Empty the box early, return it, and let the freight travel on domestic equipment that's actually sized and priced for the run.

## Where cross-docking fits

Sometimes you don't just need to change modes. You need to sort. One inbound container might carry orders for several customers, or a mix of freight that should split by region. That's cross-docking: we receive it, break it down, stage it, and reload it by destination so each truck carries only what belongs on it.

Combine that with transload and you turn a single stuck container into a set of clean outbound loads, each one going where it should on the right truck. That's a lot easier to manage than a box you keep circling back to.

## The freight recovery angle

Not every port problem is a smooth handoff. Sometimes a container has to be recovered: freight that got stranded, a load that needs to change hands, product sitting somewhere it shouldn't. Comet handles freight recovery too, so if your box is stuck in a situation that isn't textbook, there's still a way to get the goods out and moving.

## What to have ready when you call

When a container is on the clock, the faster we can plan the outbound side, the more you save. Have this handy:

1. **Container size and contents.** What's in it, how it's packed, and roughly how it's palletized or loaded.
2. **Final destinations.** One stop or several, and where they are. This tells us how to split it.
3. **Freight type.** Dry, refrigerated, oversized, or a mix. It decides the equipment.
4. **Your dates.** When the box has to go back and when the freight has to land.

With that, we can plan the transload, line up the right trucks, and give you a real picture instead of a guess.
