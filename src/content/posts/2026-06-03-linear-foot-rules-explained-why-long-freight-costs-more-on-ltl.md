You got a clean LTL quote. Then the invoice showed up and it was hundreds of dollars higher than the number you approved. The line item that did it says something like "linear foot charge" or "cubic capacity," and nobody explained it before pickup. If that has happened to you, you are not being singled out. You ran into one of the oldest rules in less-than-truckload freight, and long freight is what sets it off.

Here is what the rule actually is, why carriers apply it, and how to keep it from wrecking your budget.

## What a linear-foot charge really is

An LTL trailer is roughly 48 to 53 feet of usable deck. A carrier makes money by fitting many customers' freight onto that one trailer. Weight matters, but so does floor space. If your shipment is short, dense, and stackable, the carrier can slide other freight around it and above it. Everybody shares the trailer, and everybody pays a piece.

Long freight breaks that math. A load that runs 12, 14, or 20 feet down the trailer eats a strip of floor that nothing else can use, especially if it can't be stacked. Even if it's light, it blocks the carrier from selling that space to anyone else. So carriers wrote rules to charge for it.

The two you'll see most often:

- **Linear-foot rule.** Once your freight occupies more than a set number of linear feet of trailer floor (commonly measured once it crosses a threshold like 8 to 12 feet, depending on the carrier's tariff), the shipment gets re-rated. Instead of paying by weight and class, you get billed as if you took a much larger share of the trailer.
- **Cubic-capacity / density rule.** If your freight is bulky and light, the carrier assigns a deemed weight based on the space it fills, not what it actually weighs. A light, long shipment can be billed as if it weighed far more.

Both rules point at the same thing: floor space you took away from the carrier.

## Why it hits you as a surprise

The original quote is built off the numbers you provide: weight, dimensions, and class. If the dimensions get corrected at the dock, or the freight turns out longer than the entry, the carrier re-rates it and the linear-foot rule kicks in after the fact. That's how a $400 quote becomes a $900 bill, and why the charge feels like an ambush. It isn't fraud. It's the tariff doing exactly what it was written to do. The problem is that the rule and your freight are a bad match.

## When long freight belongs on a different trailer

Here's the position we'll take: if your freight is long enough or awkward enough to trigger linear-foot charges on an LTL trailer, that's a signal it doesn't belong on an LTL trailer. You're paying for open-deck space anyway. You might as well put it on equipment built for it.

## How to keep the charge from biting

A few habits save real money:

1. **Measure the real footprint before you quote.** Total length on the trailer floor, not just pallet count. If it crosses roughly 8 to 12 feet, ask directly whether a linear-foot rule applies.
2. **Ask which trailer the freight belongs on.** If an LTL quote comes back with a density or linear-foot flag, price a partial flatbed for comparison. The open-deck number is often lower and, more importantly, it won't move on you after pickup.
3. **Get dimensions right the first time.** Most re-rates come from length or weight that didn't match the entry. Accurate numbers up front kill most surprise bills.
4. **Bring the whole picture to someone who'll look at it.** The cheapest quote isn't the cheapest shipment if it re-rates at the dock.
