You booked the truck. The load left on time. Then it sat at the border because a form was wrong, or the broker wasn't lined up, or nobody told the driver where to go once he crossed. A shipment to Canada isn't hard because Canada is far. It's hard because it has more moving parts than a domestic move, and every one of those parts has to be right before the wheels turn.

Here's what actually trips people up, and how to move freight into Canada without the day-three phone call from a driver stuck at the crossing.

## The border is a paperwork test, not a distance problem

Most delays at the northern border have nothing to do with the truck. They come from documents that don't match. A commercial invoice that disagrees with the bill of lading, a missing description, a value that raises a question, or a customs broker who isn't ready when the driver arrives. Any one of those turns a routine crossing into a hold.

Before your load leaves the dock, you want these squared away:

- A clean commercial invoice with an accurate description, value, and country of origin.
- A bill of lading that matches the invoice line for line.
- A customs broker arranged and notified in advance, so clearance is moving before the truck shows up.
- Clear consignee and delivery details on the Canadian side, including who receives and where.

None of this is exotic. It just has to be done before, not during. The shipper who treats the border as an afterthought is the one who eats the delay.

## Open-deck and oversized loads add a second layer

If you're shipping flatbed or oversized freight across the border, you've got the customs work plus the securement and permitting work, and both sides of the line have rules. Tarping, strapping, and load securement have to hold for the whole route, not just the domestic leg. Oversized loads may need permits in the states they cross and the provinces they enter, and route restrictions don't stop at the border.

This is exactly the kind of move that gets bounced by carriers who want simple, square, domestic freight. It's also the kind of move we've been handling since 1995. If it has to ride on a deck, get strapped, tarped, and permitted, and then clear customs, that's a normal day for us. The goal is one plan that covers the load from pickup to final delivery, not a domestic carrier who hands you off at the line and wishes you luck.

## Small partial loads don't need a whole trailer

A lot of cross-border freight is a few pallets, not a full truck. If that's you, paying for a full trailer to Canada makes no sense, and big LTL networks will hit long or hard-to-handle open-deck freight with density minimums and linear-foot charges that blow up the quote. For one to several pallets of open-deck freight, partial flatbed is usually the smarter buy. ## The handoff problem, and how to skip it

The worst cross-border experiences come from too many handoffs. Freight changes hands, changes modes, and every transfer is a chance for something to fall through. A container comes off the port and has to go over the road. A load needs to be staged, broken down, and sent out for regional delivery on the Canadian or U.S. side. Every one of those steps is a place a shipment can stall.

## What to line up before you book

If you're planning a Canada move, get these answers first:

1. What's the freight, its real dimensions, weight, and value? That drives mode, permits, and paperwork.
2. Is it full truckload, partial, or a few pallets? That decides whether you want TL, partial van, or partial flatbed.
3. Who's clearing customs, and are they ready to go before the truck arrives?
4. Does it need to change modes, get staged, or get broken down before final delivery?
5. Who receives it, and what does that dock actually need?

Answer those and most of the headache disappears before it starts.
