You have a load that doesn't fit the usual boxes. Maybe it's long, maybe it's heavy, maybe it's a machine on a skid that no forklift wants to touch. And now you're staring at two words that get thrown around like they mean the same thing: flatbed and truckload. They don't. Picking the wrong one costs you money at best and a rejected pickup at worst.

Here's how we'd sort it out if you called us with an awkward shipment.

## Start with the freight, not the truck

The question isn't "what's cheaper." The question is "how does this thing actually load, ride, and unload safely?" Answer that and the mode usually picks itself.

Ask yourself:

- **Can it be enclosed?** If it fits through the doors of a dry van and can be loaded from the rear, you're probably in truckload territory.
- **Does it need to be loaded from the side or top?** Cranes, overhead loading, and forklifts working from the side mean open deck. That's flatbed.
- **Is it over dimensions?** Too tall, too wide, or too long for a van points straight to flatbed, and possibly to a specialized deck.
- **Does it need to be strapped, chained, or tarped?** That's securement work a van can't do. Flatbed.

## When truckload is the right call

Full truckload, dry or refrigerated, is your mode when the freight is enclosable and you either have enough of it to fill a trailer or you want the whole trailer to yourself for security or timing.

Reach for truckload when:

- The load fits inside a van and loads through the back.
- It's temperature-sensitive and needs a reefer.
- You want one trailer, one seal, one set of hands from pickup to delivery.
- You've got the volume to justify the space, or the freight is fragile enough that you don't want it sharing a deck.

If your volume doesn't fill a van but the freight is still enclosable, partial dry van is worth a look. You pay for the space you use instead of a whole trailer.

## When flatbed is the right call

Flatbed and open deck is the mode for anything that has to ride out in the open and get secured to a deck. This is our house specialty, so we'll be blunt about it: if it's long, heavy, oddly shaped, or has to be loaded from the side or top, a van is the wrong tool.

Reach for flatbed when:

- The freight is oversized, over-length, or over-height.
- It loads by crane or side-loading forklift.
- It needs tarping, strapping, chains, or edge protection.
- It's construction material, steel, machinery, or anything that laughs at a pallet jack.

Oversized and permitted loads are their own animal. Route restrictions, escorts, and permits turn a simple move into a project. That's exactly the kind of work we do every day, so you don't have to become an expert in state permit rules to get a transformer across three states.

## The trap in the middle: awkward but small

Here's where shippers get burned. You've got open-deck freight, but not enough to fill a flatbed. Maybe it's one long crate, or three pallets of steel. So you try to force it onto a big LTL network, and the bill comes back with charges you never approved.

That's not bad luck. Across the LTL industry, carriers apply density minimums and linear-foot charges to freight that's long, low-density, or hard to handle. A load that eats up deck length gets priced on the space it occupies, not just its weight. Long or many-pallet open-deck freight also gets rejected outright by networks that simply don't want to handle it.

When your open-deck load is too small for a full flatbed but too awkward for standard LTL, the answer is **flatbed LTL**, also called partial flatbed. You pay for the deck space you use, on a shared trailer, without the reclass-and-reweigh surprises. If that's your situation, start there.

## A quick way to decide

1. **Can it be enclosed and loaded from the rear?** If yes, look at truckload or partial van.
2. **Does it need open-deck loading, securement, or it's over dimensions?** If yes, it's flatbed.
3. **Is that flatbed load smaller than a full trailer?** Then it's flatbed LTL, not standard LTL.

Get those three answers right and you've avoided the two most expensive mistakes: paying for a whole trailer you didn't need, and handing an odd load to a network that was always going to surcharge or bounce it.
