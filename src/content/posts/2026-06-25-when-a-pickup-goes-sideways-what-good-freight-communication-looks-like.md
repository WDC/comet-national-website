A pickup falling apart is not the rare event. It's the Tuesday. The truck runs late on its last load, a driver hits a closed dock, a load gets bumped, weather stacks up appointments. You did everything right on your end, and now you're the one who has to explain to your customer why the freight isn't rolling.

The difference between a bad day and a blown relationship is almost never the missed pickup itself. It's what happens in the next hour. Here's what good communication looks like when a pickup goes sideways, and what you should expect from anyone moving your freight.

## You hear about it from us, not from your customer

The worst version of this goes like this: your consignee calls asking where the truck is, and that's how you find out it never showed. Now you're behind on information, behind on options, and you look bad to the person you're serving.

Good communication flips that. The people moving your load should be watching the pickup, and the second it's at risk, you get a call. Not a tracking page that updates six hours later. A person, telling you what they know and what they don't. Early and honest beats late and polished every time.

## We tell you what's actually happening

"There's a delay" is not information. It's a way to avoid giving you information.

What you need to hear is the real cause and the real timeline: the truck is two hours out on its prior stop, the appointment got pushed to tomorrow morning, the driver can't tarp in this rain and we're staging until it clears. When you know the actual reason, you can make your own calls. You can tell your customer something true. You can decide whether to wait or reroute.

A vague update protects the messenger. A specific one protects you.

## We show up with options, not just apologies

An apology doesn't move freight. Once we know the pickup is compromised, the conversation should already be about what we do next:

- Reschedule the pickup for the earliest realistic window and confirm it holds.
- Find alternate capacity if the original truck can't recover in time.
- Look at a different mode. If a full trailer can't get there fast, a partial might. If it's an open-deck load, our flatbed LTL side can sometimes catch a shared deck sooner.
- Adjust the downstream plan so the delivery date is still defensible, and tell you honestly if it isn't.

You shouldn't have to ask "so what are we going to do?" That should be the first thing out of our mouth.

## We own the handoffs so you don't chase them

A lot of pickups go sideways at the seams: a container coming off the port, a load that has to change modes, freight that needs to be staged and broken down before it can move. Those are the moments things fall between two parties, and the shipper ends up playing dispatcher between people who won't talk to each other.

That's part of why we run our own transload and cross-dock operation in Atlanta. When freight has to change hands or change modes, one team owns the whole sequence. You get a single point of contact instead of a phone tree, and if the plan shifts, you hear it from the same person every time.

## What to expect from anyone hauling your freight

Use this as your checklist, with us or with anyone:

1. You hear about problems from them, before your customer does.
2. They give you the real cause and a real timeline.
3. They bring options to the call, not just regrets.
4. One person owns the load, including the handoffs.
5. Nobody promises a delivery window they can't defend.

A smooth pickup tells you very little about a carrier. How they handle a bad one tells you everything.

If you've got a load coming up that you can't afford to have go quiet, call Comet and talk to a person about it before it ships. We'd rather plan the recovery we hope we never need than explain a silence after the fact.
