You approved a clean quote. The invoice came back higher. Now you are explaining to someone in accounting why the number moved, and you did not do anything wrong.

This happens because a quote is a set of assumptions, not a promise. The carrier priced your load based on what you told them and what they expected to find. When the freight, the address, or the paperwork does not match those assumptions, the price adjusts. If you know where quotes flex, you can catch the gaps before the truck ever shows up.

Here is how to read a quote so the invoice does not surprise you.

## Start with what the quote is actually pricing

Every freight quote is built on a few inputs. Read them back to yourself and ask whether each one is right:

- **Origin and destination.** Not just city and state. Is it a business with a dock, or a residential or limited-access location?
- **Weight and dimensions.** The real numbers, not a guess.
- **Freight class or commodity.** For LTL, the class drives the price. Get it wrong and the whole quote is wrong.
- **Equipment.** Van, reefer, flatbed, or partial. A load that needs a deck cannot ride in a box.
- **Services included.** Liftgate, inside delivery, appointment scheduling, and notification are usually extra, not baked in.

If any of those inputs are soft, that is where your bill will move.

## The accessorials that quietly rewrite the total

Accessorials are charges for anything beyond a standard dock-to-dock move. They are legitimate. They are also where most surprise costs live. Watch these:

- **Liftgate, residential, and limited-access.** If there is no forklift or dock on either end, the carrier brings the equipment and charges for it.
- **Reweigh and reclass.** LTL carriers weigh and inspect freight. If your numbers do not match what they find, they correct the bill to match. This is the single most common reason a quote and an invoice do not agree.
- **Detention.** If a driver waits past the free time to load or unload, the clock runs.
- **Redelivery and storage.** Miss the appointment or refuse the load, and it comes back around at a cost.

None of these are tricks. They are the difference between the load you described and the load that actually moved.

## Density and linear-foot charges: the LTL trap for long freight

If your freight is long, bulky, or light for its size, pay close attention. Across the LTL industry, carriers apply **density minimums** and **linear-foot (cubic-capacity) charges** to freight that takes up more trailer space than its weight would suggest.

Here is the mechanism. LTL trailers are shared. When your freight stretches across many feet of deck or fills a lot of cube without much weight, the carrier can no longer sell that space to anyone else. So they charge you for the footprint, not just the pounds. A quote based on weight alone can climb sharply once a long or low-density shipment gets measured. This is not a fixed multiplier, and it varies by carrier and lane, so treat any figure you are shown as an example, not a rule.

If you keep getting hit with these, that is a signal your freight does not belong in a standard LTL network. ## Questions to ask before you approve

Before you say yes to any quote, run through these:

1. **What weight and dimensions is this based on?** Confirm they match reality.
2. **What class or commodity did you use?** Make sure it fits what is actually shipping.
3. **What is not included?** Ask directly which services would add cost.
4. **What happens if the freight measures differently at pickup?** Get the answer before, not after.
5. **Is this the right mode?** The cheapest quote on the wrong equipment is not the cheapest shipment.

A quote that answers these plainly is one you can trust. A quote that goes vague on any of them is one that will surprise you later.
