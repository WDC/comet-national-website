A tradeshow booth is one of the least forgiving loads you will ever ship. The show floor opens when it opens. If your crates miss the target move-in window, you are not looking at a late fee, you are looking at an empty space where your booth should be while your team stands around in polo shirts. Then, three days later, you have to do the whole thing in reverse, on a dock that is now packed with a hundred other exhibitors trying to get out at once.

This is a deadline game more than a freight game. Here is how to move a booth so it lands on time, comes home in one piece, and doesn't bury you in surprise charges.

## The load itself is the first problem

Booth freight is awkward by nature. You've got tall crates, long counters, rolled banners, pallets of literature, and often a mix of heights and weights on the same shipment. Some of it is fragile. Some of it is oversized. A lot of it is exactly the kind of low-density, hard-to-handle freight that big LTL networks don't want.

That matters because of how LTL pricing works. When a shipment is long, light, or bulky, carriers apply density minimums and linear-foot charges. A crate that eats up eight feet of trailer but weighs almost nothing gets billed like it's much heavier, because you're paying for the space, not the scale weight. A quote that looked clean can come back reweighed and reclassed for far more than you approved. For a booth, that's common, not rare.

If your booth is only a few crates or pallets of open-deck freight, partial flatbed is usually the smarter move. That's what our sister brand **FlatbedLTL** (flatbed.delcoe.com) is built for: one to several pallets on shared deck space, with pricing that doesn't punish you for the shape of the load. When you're shipping something that a linear-foot charge would clobber, go where the pricing is honest about it.

## Move-in and move-out are their own deadlines

Show venues run on tight, scheduled windows. Most events assign a target move-in time and route inbound trucks through a marshaling yard where drivers wait their turn. Miss your window and you go to the back of the line or get turned away.

That means two things drive everything:

- **Pickup timing.** The load has to leave your dock early enough to absorb weather, traffic, and the marshaling wait, without arriving so early it sits and racks up storage.
- **A driver and dispatch who understand show freight.** Someone who knows what a marshaling yard is, has the right paperwork, and won't disappear when the schedule shifts.

This is where a single point of contact earns its keep. When the show updates a move-in time or your booth build runs long, you want one person who answers the phone and adjusts the plan, not a tracking number and a queue.

## Don't forget the trip home

The return leg is where booths get lost or damaged, and it's the part people plan the least. Move-out is chaos. Everyone is tearing down at the same time, the docks are jammed, and freight that isn't clearly labeled and scheduled for pickup gets shoved into a corner or shipped to the wrong place.

Build the return before you ever leave for the show:

1. Confirm who is crating and labeling the booth at teardown, and that the labels match the return bill of lading.
2. Lock the outbound pickup with your carrier as part of the original booking, not as an afterthought from the show floor.
3. Give your carrier the venue's move-out schedule so the truck targets the right window.
4. Confirm the final delivery point back home, including any receiving hours.

Handled this way, the return is routine instead of a scramble.

## When the booth changes modes

Some shows involve freight that arrives one way and has to leave another, or gear that comes off a container and needs to get to a venue across the country. That's a transload and cross-dock problem. Our Atlanta facility can take freight from one mode, stage it, break it down, and put it on the right truck for the show. If your booth is part of a larger shipment or coming in from overseas, that handoff can be the difference between on time and improvising.
