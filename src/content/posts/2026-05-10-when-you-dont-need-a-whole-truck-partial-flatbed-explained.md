You have three pallets of steel fittings, or a piece of equipment that runs 14 feet long, or a couple of crates that weigh more than they look. It has to ride on a deck and get strapped down. It does not fill a trailer. So what do you do?

You already know the two bad answers. You can pay for a full flatbed you don't need and eat the empty space. Or you can hand it to a big LTL network and hope the quote survives contact with reality. There's a better middle option, and it's the one most shippers don't know they can ask for.

## The trap of forcing open-deck freight into LTL

LTL pricing is built around freight that stacks, palletizes cleanly, and fits inside a box trailer. When your shipment is long, dense, or awkward, that model stops working in your favor.

Here's where the bill grows:

- **Linear-foot charges.** When freight takes up more trailer length than its weight justifies, carriers apply a cubic-capacity or linear-foot rule. A long piece that occupies a big chunk of deck gets priced like it weighs far more than it does.
- **Density minimums.** Low-density freight that eats space without the weight to match triggers a minimum charge that can dwarf your original quote.
- **Reweighs and reclasses.** The freight gets measured at a terminal, reclassified, and rebilled. The number you approved isn't the number you pay.
- **Flat-out rejection.** Plenty of big LTL networks just won't take oversized or open-deck freight. You get the "we can't handle that" call after you've already planned around it.

None of this is the carrier being difficult. It's what happens when open-deck freight goes down a lane that was never designed for it.

## What partial flatbed actually is

Partial flatbed, sometimes called flatbed LTL, is shared deck space. You pay for the room your freight takes on the trailer, not for the whole trailer. Your one to several pallets ride alongside other partial loads headed the same direction.

It's the right call when:

- Your freight needs a deck, strapping, and maybe tarping, so a dry van is out.
- You don't have enough to justify a full flatbed.
- Your shipment is long, heavy, or oddly shaped, exactly the profile that LTL networks surcharge or turn away.

Because it's priced on the space you use, you sidestep the density and linear-foot math that punishes open-deck freight in the LTL world. You get flatbed handling without paying flatbed-for-one prices.

## When partial is the wrong tool

We'd rather tell you straight than sell you the wrong service. Partial flatbed isn't always the answer.

- If your freight genuinely fills a trailer, or the timing is tight and you can't share a deck with other stops, a full truckload is the cleaner move.
- If your freight is dry, palletized, and boxable, standard LTL or partial dry van may cost you less.
- If it's a single oversized or permitted load, that's its own conversation. That kind of freight comes with securement, routing, and permit questions that deserve a real plan.

The point is matching the freight to the mode. The cheapest quote on paper isn't the cheapest shipment once accessorials land.

## How to get a clean partial flatbed quote

Good pricing starts with good information. Before you call, have this ready:

1. **Dimensions and weight.** Length, width, height, and actual weight of each piece. Long pieces especially, since length drives deck space.
2. **What it is and how it's packaged.** Crated, banded, on skids, or loose. This tells us how it secures.
3. **Whether it needs tarping.** Weather-sensitive freight changes the plan.
4. **Pickup and delivery points, plus any dock or access limits.** A tight site or no forklift matters before the truck rolls, not after.

Give us that and you get a quote that holds up, not one that unravels at a terminal.
