You asked for a number. The number came back high. Before you assume someone is padding it, know this: most quote surprises trace back to how your freight actually moves, not to a markup. If you understand the levers, you can plan around them or at least stop getting blindsided.

Here are the five reasons a quote comes in higher than you expected, and what to do about each one.

## 1. Your freight takes up more space than it weighs

Across the LTL industry, carriers price on more than pounds. Low-density freight, meaning something that fills a lot of trailer space without weighing much, triggers a density minimum. The carrier looks at the cube your shipment occupies and charges as if it weighed what that space should weigh.

If your product is bulky, boxed loosely, or stacked on oversized pallets, this is often the whole story. Tightening your packaging, using the right pallet footprint, and giving accurate dimensions up front keep the density math honest.

## 2. It's long, and long freight gets its own penalty

Linear-foot charges, sometimes called cubic-capacity rules, hit shipments that eat up a long stretch of trailer floor. Pipe, beams, lumber, framing, anything that runs past a certain length or blocks other freight from loading behind it, gets rated on the space it consumes rather than its class.

This is where LTL quotes quietly balloon. A handful of long pieces can be priced like most of a trailer. If your freight is long or hard to nest, an LTL network is often the wrong tool. ## 3. The freight class or description didn't match reality

When the paperwork says one thing and the freight says another, carriers reweigh and reclass. A reclass alone can double a bill nobody approved. It usually happens when a class was guessed, a description was vague, or dimensions were rounded down to make a quote look better.

The fix is unglamorous and reliable: weigh it, measure it, and describe it accurately the first time. A quote built on real numbers holds up. A quote built on optimistic numbers becomes a correction notice two weeks later.

## 4. Accessorials you forgot to mention

The quote you got covered dock-to-dock movement. What it may not have covered:

- A liftgate because there's no dock on one end
- A residential or limited-access location
- Inside delivery
- Appointment scheduling or a delivery window
- Storage if the freight has to wait

None of these are hidden fees. They're services, and they cost money to perform. The trouble is they rarely come up in the first phone call, so they show up on the invoice instead. When you request a quote, describe both ends of the lane honestly. It's cheaper to price it now than to dispute it later.

## 5. The cheapest quote wasn't the cheapest shipment

Sometimes a low number came back because the freight got squeezed into a mode that doesn't fit it. An odd-shaped or oversized load forced onto an LTL network may quote cheap, then rack up length and handling charges, or get rejected at pickup and rebooked in a hurry at whatever rate is available that day.

The right mode beats the biggest truck and it beats the lowest teaser rate. Open-deck freight, oversized loads, and anything that has to be strapped, tarped, or permitted belong on a deck from the start. Pricing it correctly the first time avoids the second, more expensive quote.

## How to get a quote that holds

Give whoever is quoting you the real picture: accurate weight, real dimensions, the freight class or a solid description, both pickup and delivery conditions, and any timing you're committed to. A quote built on that stays close to the final bill.
