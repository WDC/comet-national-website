Your freight can travel 1,500 miles without a hiccup and still fail in the last 100. The linehaul is the easy part. It's the final stretch, the part where a load gets broken down, staged, and delivered into a dock or a job site or a store back room, that quietly wrecks delivery dates and blows up your reputation with the customer waiting on the other end.

If you own the shipment, you already know this. The tracking says "out for delivery" and then nothing happens. The receiver has a two-hour window and the driver misses it. The freight shows up but got handled wrong somewhere in the shuffle. The last 100 miles is where most of the risk lives, and it's the part big networks care about least once your box is off the linehaul truck.

## Why the last leg is the hard leg

Long-haul is a straight line between two points. Final-mile is a hundred small decisions, and every one of them can go wrong.

- The receiver has a tight window, a liftgate requirement, or a dock that only takes certain equipment.
- The freight has to be broken down from a full trailer or a container into smaller regional runs.
- Multiple stops mean sequencing matters, and a bad route order turns a one-day job into two.
- Nobody at the delivery point wants to hear "the carrier will call you." They want to know when the truck shows up.

Across the freight industry, the final leg is where cost and complexity concentrate. It's also where a shipment stops being a number and becomes a person standing at a dock, which is exactly the moment you want a human on your side instead of a tracking page.

## Distribution starts before the truck moves

Good regional delivery isn't just driving. It's staging. When freight comes off a long-haul truck, a rail car, or an ocean container, it usually can't go straight to the final stop in the form it arrived. It has to be received, sorted, and often reworked into delivery-sized loads.

That's the work Comet does on the warehouse-and-handling side:

- **Cross-docking.** Freight comes in, gets sorted, and goes back out without sitting in long-term storage. It keeps things moving and cuts the handling that damages product.
- **Freight recovery.** When a shipment gets stuck, misrouted, or stranded, someone has to go get it and put it back in motion. That's recovery, and it saves loads that would otherwise sit.
- **Regional and local distribution.** Breaking a big inbound load down into final runs across a market, sequenced so the deliveries actually make sense.

Do the staging right and the last 100 miles gets predictable. Skip it, and you're improvising at the worst possible moment.
