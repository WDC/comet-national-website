You have a load that has to ride on a deck. Maybe it is a piece of machinery, a bundle of steel, a transformer, or a crated unit that no van will take. The first question is not price. It is which trailer actually fits, because putting the wrong trailer under your freight is how you end up with a rejected pickup, a route you cannot legally run, or a load you cannot get down to a legal height.

Here is how we think about it when someone calls with an open-deck load.

## Start with three numbers and a shape

Before we talk trailer type, we want four things:

- **Weight.** Total, and how it is distributed.
- **Height of the cargo itself**, sitting on the ground.
- **Length and width.**
- **How it loads.** Forklift, crane, or does it roll or drive on?

Those answers point to the trailer almost every time. The trap shippers fall into is picking a trailer by habit, then discovering the freight is too tall to run legally or too heavy for the axles. Get the numbers right first.

## Standard flatbed

A standard flatbed rides high, with the deck sitting up around five feet off the ground. That height is not a problem when your cargo is short. It becomes a problem when it is tall.

Standard flatbed is the right call when:

- Your freight is reasonably low, so total height stays legal once it is loaded.
- It loads from the side or top with a forklift or crane.
- Weight is within normal limits and spread across the deck.

Think building materials, steel, pipe, palletized freight that needs strapping and maybe tarping, and equipment that is wide or long but not tall. This is the workhorse, and it is the cheapest open-deck option when the load actually fits it.

## Step-deck (drop-deck)

A step-deck drops to a lower main deck behind the front section. That lower deck buys you height. Freight that would run over legal height on a standard flatbed often rides legal on a step-deck without any permit at all.

Reach for a step-deck when:

- Your cargo is tall enough that a standard flatbed would push you over height.
- You want to load with ramps, because step-decks can carry them.
- You are moving equipment or machinery that is a little too tall for flat but not tall or heavy enough to need something heavier.

Step-deck is the quiet problem-solver. A lot of loads that shippers assume need a permit just need a lower deck.

## RGN (removable gooseneck)

An RGN drops the front of the trailer to the ground so the freight can be driven or rolled straight on. The well between the axles sits very low, which gives you the most height clearance of the three. RGNs also carry serious weight, often across multiple axles.

An RGN is the answer when:

- Your load is tall, and a step-deck still would not get it legal.
- It is very heavy and needs more axles to spread the weight.
- It is self-propelled or rolls: dozers, excavators, and large drivable equipment.

RGN moves are where permits, routing, securement, and sometimes escorts come into play. This is not a load you want handed to a carrier who does open-deck once in a while.

## When the load pushes past standard equipment

Oversized and heavy loads bring their own layer: state permits, legal travel times, route restrictions around bridges and low clearances, and escort requirements. This is the part that stresses shippers out, and for good reason. One missed permit or a route with a bridge you cannot clear turns a shipment into a standstill.

Sometimes the load is only a few pieces or a handful of pallets of open-deck freight. You do not need a dedicated trailer for that, and you should not pay for one. ## The short version

- **Standard flatbed:** low freight, normal weight, side or top load.
- **Step-deck:** taller freight that needs to stay legal without a permit.
- **RGN:** the tallest and heaviest loads, and anything that drives or rolls on.
